# Section patterns

A toolbox of distinctive ways to present each part of a portfolio. These are options, not a checklist; pick what fits the person and their field. The unifying idea: a structural choice should encode something true about the content, not just decorate it. Default card grids and bullet lists are the baseline to beat.

## Hero

The hero is a thesis. Open with the most characteristic thing in the person's world, not a job title and a stock photo.

- **Name plus thesis line.** Big name for recognition, with a one-line point of view underneath (italic display works well). The thesis should be graspable by a non-specialist in a second.
- **Value, not mechanism.** Describe what they do by what it does for others. "I build the data layer when none exists yet" beats "Data Engineer with 5 years' experience."
- **One memorable element on the side.** Not a generic illustration. Consider a *content-native* object: for an engineer, themselves rendered as a typed record or struct ("self as a record"); for a writer, a pull-quote set like a book page; for a designer, a single live specimen. Type-led, employer-agnostic, no clip-art.
- **Minimal outward action.** Two buttons at most: the primary is "Get in touch"; a secondary can scroll to the work. Avoid a résumé download as the main CTA when the site itself is the résumé.

Keep employer names out of the hero if the person wants the site to outlast any one job.

## About

Replace the single grey paragraph with two parts:

- **A short, human bio** in their voice, opened by a strong pull-line (a second thesis the hero didn't use). Name their actual path; keep it employer-agnostic in framing if they asked for that, while still naming real history.
- **A "what I do" band.** Turn their core themes into 3 or 4 parallel areas, each a short title plus a one-liner. A dark contrast band here gives the page its first change of texture. Only number them (01 to 04) if they're truly a sequence; parallel themes shouldn't imply an order.

## Experience

The strongest upgrade over a bullet list is **show your working**: frame each role as **Problem, Built, Outcome**.

- *Problem*: the gap that existed.
- *Built*: what they put in place.
- *Outcome*: what it moved. The number is the payoff, not the headline.

This makes the reader *watch* the person be rigorous rather than be told they are. Lay roles on a timeline rail. Use a quiet visual hierarchy (for example a bold accent on the major recent roles, a muted accent on early internships) so seniority reads at a glance. Not every role splits cleanly into the three parts, so offer a simpler one-line treatment for minor early roles, and let the person choose full versus simple per role.

Mono-set the company, role, and date label so it reads like metadata.

## Skills

Avoid four equal boxes of comma-lists. Give the grouping *meaning*:

- **Layered stack.** Present skills as strata from foundation to growing edge (for example languages at bedrock, up through tools and platforms, to the cutting-edge things they're sharpening now). This reads the way the person thinks about a system, and the top "growing edge" layer signals trajectory, not just inventory. Works for any field with a natural foundation-to-frontier structure.
- **By reach-for.** Group by "what I use when..." rather than by category, if that's truer to how they work.
- Highlight the current frontier in the accent colour; keep the rest neutral. A tighter list reads more senior than an exhaustive one, so propose cuts.

## Projects / work

- Use a distinct accent for this section so it doesn't blur into experience.
- Each card: title, one-line problem or result, and a mono tech or medium tag.
- Feature one card (a stronger accent) if one piece is the strongest or most real-world.
- Resolve duplication: if a project also appears as a role, keep it in one place.
- For deep work, a project can graduate to its own case-study page later (see build-and-deploy for routing).

## Education and recognition

- Education as two clean records (school, mono meta line, one-line detail). Add it even if the old site omitted it.
- Pull awards and wins into a single scannable **recognition strip** (short pills with a subtle accent) rather than burying them in prose. A competition win can live both in the timeline and the strip.

## Contact

- One clear ask, warm and specific: name the kinds of conversations they want.
- Primary action is email; secondary are profile links.
- Keep the footer employer-agnostic if the hero is, so the whole site travels with them.

## Cross-cutting devices

- **A through-line motif.** Reuse one idea across sections so the site feels like one thought (for example "self as a record" in the hero rhyming with "each tool as a record" elsewhere). One motif, used two or three times, beats scattered cleverness.
- **Mono for metadata.** Setting labels, dates, and data in a mono face quietly signals precision and separates metadata from prose.
- **Contrast bands.** Alternate light and one dark band to give a long scroll rhythm; don't make every section the same surface.
- **Restraint.** Spend boldness in one place (the signature). Before shipping, remove one accessory: cut the decoration that doesn't serve the brief.
